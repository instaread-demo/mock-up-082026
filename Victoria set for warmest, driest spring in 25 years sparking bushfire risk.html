Victoria set for warmest, driest spring in 25 years sparking bushfire risk

1 day ago

Victorian fire authorities are bracing for one of the worst bushfire seasons in decades, with new predictions of a warm and dry spring.

East Gippsland, far south-west Victoria, the Surf Coast, and the area west of Geelong to the southern parts of the Wombat Forest are at an elevated risk of bushfires from October.

More than 436,000 hectares of land across Victoria was burnt in bushfires in 2025.

The Australasian Fire Authorities Council's (AFAC) latest seasonal outlook has prompted warnings from fire authorities that a heightened fire risk is "the new normal".

Low rain, warmer temperatures

The AFAC report shows in south-west Victoria, leftover dead vegetation from prolonged drought conditions is still present even with an increase in soil moisture.

Forest Fire Management Victoria chief fire officer Chris Hardman, said the organisation had not seen a similar outlook since the early 2000s.

"We can go back to 2002, 2003, they were similar conditions," Mr Hardman said.

"It wasn't dissimilar either in 2006 and 2007, and they were the years that we had those large landscape-scale fires."

"We are looking at really significant underlying rainfall deficits," he said.

"If that doesn't change through spring then we can expect to see some early onset bushfire season activity,

"We've all got our fingers and toes crossed that we will get a spring break and we will get some rain," Mr Hardman said.

Rainfall since April has been below average across East Gippsland, but the rest of the state has recorded average to above-average rain.

The Bureau of Meteorology's forecasts show below-average rainfall is likely across parts of southern and eastern Australia.

Australia is expected to see a very likely chance of unusually high maximum temperatures from September to November.

Significant grass growth is also expected in the north and west of the state.

Australia has seen record warm temperatures through winter.

"One of the really challenging things we are seeing is that even though we have those rainfall deficits, we have had really good rain in the Western districts," Mr Hardman said.

"That means we have a bumper harvest, which is great for farmers … but we could see some of those hard-running grass or crop fires in the north-west, the west, and down to central Victoria."

Mr Hardman said "it certainly appears" that repeated high bushfire predictions may be the new normal.

"Certainly in the last three years we've had above-average bushfire seasons. This is no different," he said.

"With climate change, and on average we are getting about 15 per cent less spring rain than we used to get back in the 1970s and 1980s, and without the spring rain, that inevitably leads to higher-than-average bushfire seasons."

Australia's climate drivers

Speaking this morning at a bushfire briefing, general manager of National Production Service, Michael Logan said, reiterated a low-rain forecast into spring. 

"The long-range forecasts suggests that in spring, rainfall is likely to be below average across south eastern and eastern Australia," Mr Logan said.

"The other thing that is clear in the signal for the season ahead is a continuation of higher temperatures both in the daytime and overnight as well."

Victoria's water storages and catchments are "at a similar level" to the same time last year.

The Bureau of Meteorology's long-range forecasts incorporates influence from Australia's surrounding oceans, and atmosphere.

"That does include things like the state of the El Niño , the Southern Oscillation Index, what’s happening with the Indian ocean Dipole as well as other long-term climate trends," Mr Logan said.

"I'm sure most people are aware… an El Nino event is underway and it is strengthening and it is going to be a very strong one."

Emergency Management Victoria Commissioner Tim Wiebusch urged property owners to begin preparations early.

"Our emergency services [in Victoria] are beginning to prepare. We're undertaking exercises, our pre-season briefings have commenced, but we now need Victorians to also start their preparations," Mr Wiebusch said.

"Start clearing up around your homes, clean your gutters - whether it's to prevent ember attack or flooding that might come through from a storm."
