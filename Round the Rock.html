August 12 Planetary Alignment: Three Sky Shows in One Day

Wednesday, August 12 is getting called the biggest skywatching day of the year, and most of the coverage you will see is about a total solar eclipse. Here is the honest local version: Round Rock will not see that eclipse. What we do get is still very good, and it happens in the dark hours on either end of the day.

The August 12 planetary alignment puts six planets across our morning sky before sunrise. That same night, the Perseid meteor shower peaks under a brand new moon, which lines up on a peak night only once every couple of decades. Both are free, both work from a driveway, and neither one needs a telescope.

Before sunrise: six planets over Round Rock

Jupiter, Mercury, Mars, Uranus, Saturn, and Neptune will all sit above the horizon at the same time on the morning of August 12. Sunrise in Round Rock is 6:55 a.m., so the window that matters is roughly 6:10 to 6:40 a.m. Go out much earlier and the low planets have not cleared the horizon yet. Wait much later and the sky washes out.

One expectation to set before you go: this is not a tidy row of dots. The planets spread in a wide arc from the eastern horizon across to the southwest.

What to look for, and where

  • Mars sits high in the east, about a third of the way up the sky. Naked eye, faintly orange, the easiest one to find.
  • Saturn is high in the southwest and the steadiest bright point in that part of the sky. Naked eye.
  • Mercury rises around 5:46 a.m. in the east-northeast and stays low. Bright, but it needs a clear horizon.
  • Jupiter clears the horizon around 6:10 a.m., also east-northeast. It is the brightest of the six and the hardest to catch, because it hugs the treeline the whole time.
  • Uranus is high in the east. You will want binoculars.
  • Neptune is in the southwest and needs a telescope.

Those two low ones are the whole challenge. Pick a spot with an open view to the east-northeast, clear of trees and rooflines. And the mornings on either side of August 12 work nearly as well, so if Wednesday clouds over, try Tuesday or Thursday.

The eclipse is real. It is just not ours.

A total solar eclipse does happen on August 12, and it is a big one: the first total eclipse visible from mainland Europe since 1999. The path of totality crosses far Arctic Russia, Greenland, Iceland, northern Spain, the Balearic Islands, and a small corner of northeastern Portugal.

A partial eclipse reaches parts of the northeastern United States, the Mid-Atlantic, and the Upper Midwest that afternoon. It does not reach Texas. Not partially, not a sliver. From here, the sun and moon never get close enough together in our sky for anything to happen at all.

We are spelling that out because a lot of national coverage will not. If you see a post telling you to grab eclipse glasses on August 12, that advice is written for somebody else’s zip code.

And while we are on it: no, gravity is not switching off for a few seconds that day. That one goes viral before every eclipse and it has never once been true.

After midnight: the Perseids at their best

This is the one to plan around.

The Perseid meteor shower peaks the night of August 12 into the early morning of August 13. What makes 2026 different is the timing. The new moon lands at 12:36 p.m. on August 12, so the peak arrives under a sky with no moonlight in it whatsoever. According to Star Walk, the next time a Perseid peak falls on a new moon night is 2045.

The catch is that the meteors appear to radiate from the constellation Perseus, which sits low in our northeastern sky for most of the evening. Before midnight, you will see very few. From Round Rock, here is roughly how the night builds:

  • 10 p.m. to midnight: the radiant is still near the horizon. Expect a handful of long, slow meteors skimming sideways across the sky.
  • Midnight to 2 a.m.: steadily improving. Good time to head out if you have not already.
  • 2 a.m. to dawn Thursday: the real window. The radiant is halfway up the sky and still climbing.

Under genuinely dark skies the Perseids can throw off around 100 meteors an hour at peak. From a lit street in town you will see a fraction of that, which is why getting away from streetlights does more for you than any piece of equipment.

How to actually do this

  • Leave the telescope. Meteors are a naked-eye event, and four of the six planets are too. Binoculars are the only upgrade that helps.
  • Give your eyes 20 to 30 minutes to adjust. No phone screen during that stretch. This is the step everybody skips and then wonders why they saw nothing.
  • Lie back. A camp chair or a blanket on the driveway beats standing there craning your neck.
  • Look up, not at Perseus. Meteors show up all over the sky, and the ones farthest from the radiant leave the longest trails.
  • Skip the eclipse glasses. Nothing here needs them.

As for where: anywhere with a wide, dark view. Our guide to Round Rock parks is a decent starting point, though check posted hours before you plan a 2 a.m. visit, since most city parks close overnight. Honestly, a backyard with the porch light off, or a short drive out past the neighborhood glow, will get you most of the way there.

If you get a good shot, send it our way. We would love to share what our neighbors caught.
