Beyond One Senate Race: What AFGE’s Endorsement Reveals About Federal Employee Unions

AFGE's endorsement of Abdul El-Sayed highlights the political role of federal employee unions and raises questions about representing a non-partisan workforce.

The American Federation of Government Employees (AFGE), the nation’s largest federal employee union, has endorsed Abdul El-Sayed for the U.S. Senate following his narrow victory in Michigan’s Democratic primary. Local AFGE units, including Local 704 (representing EPA workers) and Local 2092, had already backed him during the primary. His views have generated many comments about his impact on and the future of the Democratic Party. Will his endorsement by a major federal employee union have any impact on the federal workforce, which is often described as non-partisan?

For federal employees, the more important question is not the ideological tag. It is what the endorsement reveals about the increasingly political role of federal employee unions and what El-Sayed’s election could mean for the federal workforce.

No other major federal employee unions—such as the National Treasury Employees Union (NTEU), the National Federation of Federal Employees (NFFE), or the International Federation of Professional and Technical Engineers (IFPTE)—appear to have issued comparable endorsements for El-Sayed in this race, based on available public statements. His wider labor support has come primarily from private-sector and other public-sector unions, including the UAW, National Nurses United, AFA-CWA, and AFT Michigan, as well as the American Association of University Professors (AAUP).

The AFGE endorsement itself is not a surprise. AFGE has usually supports candidates, primarily Democrats, who favor expanding collective bargaining rights and strengthening organized labor. What makes it noteworthy is El-Sayed’s identification with the far-left progressive wing of the Democratic Party and his support regarding policies frequently associated with democratic socialism—Medicare for All, expanded government programs, stronger labor unions, and greater redistribution—while he personally says he rejects the socialist label.

El-Sayed has stated he is “simply not a socialist,” that he is “not somebody who is a socialist,” and that he “believe[s] in capitalism” that “has to be regulated.” He is not a member of the Democratic Socialists of America (DSA). However, he has spoken at DSA events in the past and received endorsements from Sen. Bernie Sanders, Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez, and has campaigned with members of the Squad. Critics and some progressive observers routinely apply the democratic-socialist label because the substance of his platform is similar to positions of the DSA.

Why AFGE Supports El-Sayed

AFGE’s endorsement shows a clear alignment of policy goals. During the Trump administration, AFGE has challenged a number of executive orders and personnel policies that reduced collective bargaining rights, limited official time, narrowed grievance procedures, and made it easier for agencies to reduce staffing.

El-Sayed advocates policies that go in the opposite direction. He supports expanding labor protections, strengthening unions (including passage of the PRO Act), increasing public spending, and giving employees greater workplace protections. From AFGE’s perspective, those positions are consistent with the union’s mission of protecting and expanding the rights of federal employees.

What Would His Election Mean?

If elected, El-Sayed would be one of 100 senators. A freshman senator has limited ability to reshape federal personnel policy on his own. However, senators vote on legislation affecting federal labor-management relations, civil service protections, federal employee pay and benefits, and the confirmation of nominees to agencies such as the Federal Labor Relations Authority, Merit Systems Protection Board, and Office of Personnel Management.

Should political control in Washington shift in favor of Democrats, senators with views similar to El-Sayed’s would likely support legislation restoring bargaining rights reduced in recent years and strengthening statutory protections for federal employees. His platform also includes stronger antitrust enforcement and measures to protect multi-employer pensions—areas of interest to many federal workers and their unions.

Additional Implications for the Federal Workforce

Two additional areas deserve attention from federal employees:

Healthcare Benefits

Medicare for All is a centerpiece of El-Sayed’s campaign. A single-payer model would largely replace private insurance, including the Federal Employees Health Benefits (FEHB) program.

El-Sayed has told union audiences that workers could retain employer or union plans as add-ons or supplements. In contrast, a public plan provides guaranteed coverage without premiums, copays, or deductibles.

For federal employees who currently enjoy relatively comprehensive FEHB options, the change could lower out-of-pocket costs for some while altering negotiated plan designs, provider networks, and the overall value of existing benefits. Unions would still bargain over supplements, but the baseline would shift.

Agency Missions and Size of Government

Progressive priorities, including expanded social programs, larger public-health investment, and immigration-enforcement restructuring, generally point toward larger federal involvement. El-Sayed’s call to abolish Immigration and Customs Enforcement (while supporting certain forms of border enforcement) would directly affect DHS components and their employees. Broader proposals involving wealth taxes and public investment could increase demand for administrators, researchers, and regulators while raising longer-term questions about fiscal sustainability and potential trade-offs involving pay and benefits.

The Larger Story: Federal Unions Becoming More Political

Many observers see this as a practical necessity. Presidential administrations, Congress, and the courts increasingly shape personnel policies affecting federal employees.

Unions argue they must participate in elections to protect their members’ interests.

Critics reach a different conclusion. They argue that federal employee unions increasingly resemble political advocacy organizations rather than organizations focused primarily on workplace representation. From that perspective, endorsing candidates with strong ideological positions, such as those espoused by Al-Sayed, risks alienating members whose political views differ from the union’s leadership.

A Non-Partisan Federal Workforce—or Partisan Representation?

One of the most interesting questions raised by this endorsement concerns a phrase frequently used in debates over federal personnel policy: the “non-partisan federal workforce.” Federal employee unions, public employee organizations, and many elected officials often describe career federal employees as non-partisan, particularly when arguing against policies supported by the Trump administration.

The phrase is commonly invoked when opposing proposals that would make it easier to remove employees or when arguing that career civil servants should be insulated from political influence. Unions and other federal employee organizations often state the federal workforce is non-political. For example, here is a recent example from NARFE:

The nonpartisan, merit-based civil service provides continuity through changing administrations and ensures public servants’ primary allegiance is to the Constitution and laws of the United States rather than the individual temporarily holding the office of President. (emphasis added)

Without any sense of irony, federal unions have frequently stated that proposals from the Trump administration threaten the non-political workforce structure.

There is little dispute that the federal civil service is intended to be politically neutral in carrying out the laws enacted by Congress and policies established by elected officials, regardless of which party controls the White House.

But while the workforce itself is expected to remain non-partisan in performing its duties, the organizations representing many federal employees are under no similar obligation. AFGE’s endorsement of El-Sayed illustrates that distinction.

The union is exercising its First Amendment rights and advancing the political priorities it believes best serve its members. Yet endorsements of candidates identified with one end of the political spectrum inevitably raise questions about whether union leadership reflects the diversity of political views among the employees it represents.

That distinction may become increasingly important as debates carry on over civil service reform, union rights, reductions in force, and the proper role of career federal employees.

A Larger Issue for Federal Employees

The endorsement raises several questions that deserve serious consideration by federal employees regardless of their political views.

  • Does endorsing candidates with strong progressive or democratic-socialist-adjacent identities strengthen a union’s ability to protect its members, or does it make the union appear more partisan than representative?
  • Do political endorsements reflect the views of the majority of union members, or primarily the priorities of union leadership?
  • Will closer association with one political party improve unions’ ability to alter future administrations—or make relations with administrations from the opposing party more difficult?
  • How would a shift toward Medicare for All interact with existing FEHB coverage and union-negotiated health plans?
  • Finally, how should federal employee unions balance political advocacy with their primary responsibility of representing employees in grievances, negotiations, disciplinary actions, and workplace issues?

Reasonable people will answer these questions differently. But AFGE’s endorsement of Abdul El-Sayed is another indication that the debate over the future of the federal civil service increasingly goes beyond personnel policy into the wider political field.

As federal employee unions become more active participants and advocates in national politics, federal employees themselves may ultimately decide whether that evolution strengthens—or weakens—the organizations that represent them.
